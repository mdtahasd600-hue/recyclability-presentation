Member 1 • Opening

1. What Is Nature’s Harmony?

  • Nature is a connected system in which different orders exist together.
  • The material, plant, animal and human orders depend on one another.
  • Nature shows repeated cycles instead of one-way consumption.
  • Resources are continuously transformed and reused in different forms.
  • These cycles help maintain continuity and balance on Earth.
  • UHV calls this cyclic character recyclability in nature.
  • Along with it, nature also demonstrates self-regulation.

Interconnectedness → Cycles → Balance → Continuity

2. Recyclability: Nature’s Circular System

  • Recyclability means materials participate in recurring natural cycles.
  • What appears to be “waste” in one process can become a resource for another.
  • Water moves through evaporation, condensation and precipitation.
  • Carbon moves among atmosphere, organisms, soil and other reservoirs.
  • Nutrients can return to soil through decomposition.
  • These processes support continuity rather than permanent accumulation.
  • Thus, nature works largely through circulation and transformation.
Member 2 • Water Cycle

3. Water Cycle: A Simple Example of Recyclability

  • Solar energy causes water to evaporate from oceans, lakes and other surfaces.
  • Water vapour rises and cools, leading to condensation.
  • Clouds form and water returns through precipitation such as rain or snow.
  • Water then flows through rivers, soil and groundwater systems.
  • Plants also return water to the atmosphere through transpiration.
  • The same water therefore participates in many stages again and again.
  • This illustrates how a natural cycle can maintain continuity of a resource.

Member 2 • Nutrient Cycles

4. Carbon, Oxygen & Nitrogen: Nature Reuses

  • Plants absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and release oxygen.
  • Animals and humans use oxygen during respiration and return carbon dioxide.
  • Decomposition returns nutrients and carbon-containing materials to ecosystems.
  • Nitrogen is converted into usable forms through biological and soil processes.
  • Plants take nutrients from soil and animals obtain them through food.
  • When organisms die, decomposers help return matter to the environment.
  • These cycles connect organisms with air, water and soil.

Member 3 • Self-Regulation

5. What Is Self-Regulation?

⚖️
Balance without a central controller
  • Self-regulation means natural systems maintain appropriate relationships within themselves.
  • It does not mean nature never changes or never faces disturbance.
  • Instead, many interactions tend to maintain continuity and workable balance.
  • Population, food availability and environmental conditions influence one another.
  • In a forest, plants, animals, soil, water and climate are interconnected.
  • Food chains and competition can prevent unlimited growth of a single population.
  • Thus, nature shows an organised order without one human manager controlling it.
Member 3 • Forest Example

6. Self-Regulation in a Forest Ecosystem

  • Plants depend on sunlight, water, minerals, soil and suitable conditions.
  • Herbivores depend on plants, while predators depend on other animals.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil.
  • Predation and competition influence population sizes.
  • Availability of food and habitat affects reproduction and survival.
  • No single species can grow without limits in a finite ecosystem.
  • The network of relationships contributes to the continuity of the forest.

Member 4 • Four Orders in UHV

8. Four Orders: Mutual Fulfilment

  • Material Order: soil, water, air, minerals and physical resources.
  • Plant/Bio Order: trees, crops, grass and other plants.
  • Animal Order: animals, birds, insects and other organisms.
  • Human Order: human beings with body and the capacity for understanding.
  • Material conditions support plant and animal life.
  • Plants provide food, oxygen and habitats, while animals contribute to ecological processes.
  • UHV emphasises understanding these relationships as mutual fulfilment.

Member 5 • Human Responsibility

9. What Happens When We Break the Cycles?

  • Human activities can extract resources faster than natural systems can replenish them.
  • Persistent waste can accumulate when it does not readily re-enter natural cycles.
  • Pollution can disturb air, water, soil and living organisms.
  • Deforestation can remove habitats and interrupt ecological relationships.
  • Overuse of resources can reduce the resilience of ecosystems.
  • The issue is not that nature has lost all self-regulation, but that human actions can strongly disturb natural processes.
  • Therefore, responsible production and consumption become important.

Member 5 • Practical Application

10. What Can We Learn From Nature?

♻️ Reduce → Reuse → Repair → Recycle

Design products for longer life and recovery of materials.

🌱 Protect natural cycles

Save water, soil, biodiversity and habitats.

  • Design production systems that minimise waste and recover useful materials.
  • Prefer durable, repairable and reusable products where practical.
  • Separate waste so recyclable and organic materials can be processed appropriately.
  • Use resources efficiently rather than treating them as unlimited.
  • Protect forests, wetlands and other ecosystems that perform natural cycles.
  • Think of humans as participants in nature, not separate from it.
  • Human values can support choices that aim at mutual fulfilment.
